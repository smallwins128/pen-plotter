The design in one paragraph

Three supplies collapse into one

The box you sketched had three PSUs. It needs one. The Arduino runs on USB and must keep its USB data link to the Mac regardless, so a second 5 V source only fights its regulator. The endstops are bare switches to ground — GRBL's internal pull-ups power them, so they need no supply at all. And the servo's 6 V has to come from a DC-DC converter in any case, because nobody makes a 6 V mains PSU.

So: one 24 V brick, one fused distribution block, one 0 V star point. Common grounds stop being something you wire and become something the topology guarantees. The only judgement call left is where the servo's step-down lives — and moving it out of the box to the carriage is the single most useful thing in this whole design.

Panel

Where the 19 wires land

Six ports carry everything. Steppers get GX16 shells (16 mm hole), endstops and pen get GX12 (12 mm) — different shell diameters, so a stepper lead physically cannot be pushed into the pen port. That matters more than a label, because the pen port carries 24 V on pin 1 and a servo plugged into it dies instantly.

Interior

What sits where

Why the PSU sits at the back

It frees the whole front face for connectors. Put the supply along the left end instead and six ports have to crowd into what is left, which is under 30 mm centres — closer than a GX16 backshell will allow you to tighten.

Two supplies work, and the box follows whichever you pick. The tool takes --psu lrs100 or --psu lrs350 and re-lays the whole thing.

Not LRS-50-24: at 2.2 A against a 2.0 A load there is no margin at all. Not LRS-75-24 either — 3.2 A is enough, but the case is only 30 mm shorter than the LRS-100 and the box it produces crowds the port row for nothing.

The one non-obvious move

Regulate at the servo, not in the box

Your open fault is a controller reset when the servo actuates after a move, and the standing suspect is 1.5 m of thin wire between the buck and an MG996R. Centralising power in an enclosure makes that run longer. So don't send 6 V up the umbilical — send 24 V, and put the step-down on the carriage.

Still 3 wires

If you later want the last few dB, run a 4-core and give the PWM its own return straight to a shield GND pin. At 0.7 A the shared-return offset is tens of millivolts against a 5 V logic threshold, so 3 cores is genuinely fine — it was the 2.5 A version of that same wire that was killing you.

5 V or 6 V? Either, and a fixed 5 V 3 A module is arguably the better pick. Stall current scales with supply voltage over winding resistance, so the MG996R's ~2.5 A at 6 V becomes roughly 2.1 A at 5 V — a smaller transient in exactly the wire that has been resetting the board. The cost is torque, about 9.4 kg·cm instead of 11, against a requirement of 1.2 for a 30 mm crank or 0.10 for the 2.5 mm cam. Order of magnitude in hand either way. The 2200 µF still goes at the servo with any regulator: it is the cap, not the converter, that supplies the lift.

Assembly

Build order, each step ending in a check

  1. Drill and dry-fit, no wire

    Every hole: 16 mm × 3, 12 mm × 2, the USB cutout, the IEC module's rectangle, the 60 mm fan and its four M4s on 50 mm centres. Drop all six connectors in loose.

    Check: every backshell tightens without fouling its neighbour, and the lid still closes over the driver heatsinks.

  2. Mains, complete and covered, before any DC

    0.75 mm² with ferrules on every conductor. Inlet L/N to the PSU input, inlet earth to the PE block, PE block to the PSU earth terminal and to the box metalwork. Shroud the inlet and PSU input terminals.

    Check: plug in, switch on, meter 24 V at the PSU output, switch off. Nothing else is connected yet.

  3. DC distribution and the star point

    +V to the +24 block, −V to the 0 V block, F1 and F2 in their branches. Land every return on the block itself — no daisy chains between loads.

    Check: continuity from each 0 V landing to the block; no continuity from 0 V to PE.

  4. Board in, bulk cap at its terminals

    Shield V+ and GND on 0.75 mm², with the 220–470 µF 50 V electrolytic soldered or clamped right at the screw terminals. Short USB-B lead from the Uno to the panel socket. Re-fit the D12 jumper.

    Check: USB only, 24 V still off — $I returns [VER:1.1h.plotter-servo-1:].

  5. Endstops

    Two-pair screened cable into LIM, drain to shield GND at the box end alone.

    Check: ? with nothing pressed shows no Pn: field at all. Press each switch by hand — Pn:X, then Pn:Y, each clearing on release. Do this before homing.

  6. Pen port and the carriage pod

    Build the pod first: UBEC, 2200 µF 16 V with its striped leg to 0 V, legs trimmed short, anchored so it cannot flap. Set the UBEC to 6.0 V with the servo disconnected, meter on the output.

    Check: T0 — five clean up/down cycles, no buzzing at either end, no reset.

  7. Motors last

    Switch off at the panel for every single plug and unplug. A panel connector makes hot-plugging easy, and hot-plugging a stepper is still the fastest way to kill an A4988.

    Check: both X motors turn together on T1. If they fight, swap one coil pair on one motor — not $3, which flips the whole axis.

  8. Fan, lid, and back to the ladder

    Fan blowing in across the drivers, exhausting through the vents at the PSU end.

    Check: T1 through T6 pass as before, and the drivers are warm rather than hot after a long plot.

Fabrication

One blank folds into the box

Floor and all four walls come off one piece of 1.5 mm steel. The lid is a second, shallower pan that telescopes over it. The base carries no component-specific mounting holes at all — just a field of Ø3.5 on a 25 mm grid, which is the pitch of DIN rail's own slots.

That grid is what makes the dimensions you don't have yet survivable. Anything whose hole pattern doesn't land on it — the Uno's four holes never will, they aren't on any grid and none of the spacings is round — gets a small flat adapter plate cut from the offcut. Being wrong about a component costs you a plate, not a box.

The shop's bend deduction wins

2.654 mm falls out of R = 1.5, t = 1.5, K = 0.42 — and K belongs to whoever owns the press brake. Every hole in a wall shifts in the flat if their number differs from ours. Hand them the folded dimensions and let them do their own unfold; use the DXFs for cutouts and hole positions, or as a cross-check. If they want the flat as-is, tell them what deduction it assumes.

tools/enclosure_dxf.py writes both blanks, the adapter plates and an SVG preview, with every dimension at the top of the file. It self-checks each run — no two holes leaving under 1.5 mm of web, nothing within 3 mm of a bend line — so when you replace a MEASURE figure with a real one, the run tells you whether it still fits.

Before you order anything

Two things this box does not do

It does not fix the servo reset. Do the cap at the servo, the twisted pairs and the shortened arm on the machine as it stands, and confirm the fault is gone. Building an enclosure around an unsolved fault means debugging it through a lid.

It does not change the power-up order — and doesn't need to. The panel switch cuts 24 V only; USB arrives separately from the Mac. Plug USB first so GRBL boots and drives the enable line and D11 to a known state, then flip the switch. Down in reverse. That rocker is also your emergency stop, and it is the only one that works when the terminal has stopped listening.
