DIN rail parts

Everything that goes on the two rails, what the specification has to say, and how many. The quantities come from the same tables as the two layout drawings, so they cannot disagree with them.

The thing worth understanding before you order: you never wire one terminal block to the next. Blocks that should be the same node are bridged by a comb that drops into a slot they all share. That is what a common bar is, and it is why the two boxes need only 79 wires between them instead of ninety-three.

What one block is

How you make a common bar

Worked example, the ground group in the control box. Five blocks sit side by side: 0V 0Va 0Vb 0Vc 0Vd. One 5-way comb bridges all five. Nine wires land on them — one in from the power box and eight out to the drivers, the board, the servo, the two endstop commons and the fan — and none of those nine wires joins one block to another. Every one of them goes somewhere real. Without the comb the same job needs four extra link wires and the ground star stops being a star.

Specifications that actually matter

  1. Pitch, and only one of them. Every block, every comb, every end cover is 5.2 mm pitch, which is what a 2.5 mm² block is. Mixing pitches means the combs fit nothing.
  2. One family, one brand. Combs, end covers and marker strips are not interchangeable between manufacturers, and often not between series from the same manufacturer. Pick a family and buy the accessories with the blocks. This is the mistake that costs a second order.
  3. 7.5 mm rail, not 15 mm. Both are called TS35. Terminal blocks want the 7.5 mm one; the deep rail is for heavy gear.
  4. Current rating is not the constraint. A 2.5 mm² block is good for 24 A and the heaviest thing here is 1.7 A. Size blocks by the wire you can get into them, not by the current.
  5. Push-in beats screw for a first build. Screw clamps need the right torque and want re-checking after the box has warmed and cooled a few times. Push-in takes a ferruled wire with a shove and holds it. Either works; push-in is less to get wrong.

Things that catch people out

  1. The last block in a row is open on one side. Its clamp is exposed metal. That is what the end cover is for, and it is the part everyone forgets.
  2. Earth blocks are commoned by the rail. They have a metal foot that grips it, so every earth block on that rail is already one node — no comb, and the incoming earth must land on one of them for the rail to be live with earth at all. Keep the rail one unbroken piece.
  3. A comb is a bus bar, so treat it like one. Nothing stops you combing two groups together by accident. Count the ways twice before you push it home; cut it short rather than long.
  4. Ferrule everything stranded. A bare stranded end in a clamp splays, and the strand that escapes is the one that finds its neighbour.
  5. Label as you go. The marker strips are cheap and the rail schedules on the two layout pages are the names to write. Six months from now the colours will not be enough.
Order of assembly. Rail cut, deburred and screwed down first. Then end clamp, blocks pushed on in schedule order, second end clamp. Then the combs, then the end covers, then the markers — and only then start landing wires. Fitting a comb after the wires are in means taking wires out.
